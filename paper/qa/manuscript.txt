009                                                    A BSTRACT
010
011             Learning to predict physical fields requires fine simulation data that can be
012             expensive to obtain. Multifidelity learning combines inexpensive coarse solutions
013             with fine data to improve generalization to new parameter inputs, but the
014             most effective way to use these fidelities varies across problems. We present
015             AUTO MF, an automated ensemble workflow for multifidelity field prediction. We
016             construct its library by adapting neural operators, convolutional networks, and
017
                reduced basis models into nine parameter to field surrogates. For each dataset,
                five additional input and fine solution pairs, reserved from surrogate training,
018
                guide model selection and ensemble weighting. The workflow either selects an
019
                individual model, weights models according to their individual errors, or jointly
020             fits weights to minimize the combined prediction error. Each model receives
021             one weight that remains fixed across the field and across new inputs, allowing
022             prediction without an additional coarse simulation. We assemble a benchmark of
023             22 datasets spanning seven classes of partial differential equations (PDEs) and
024             climate emulation, combining our simulations with imported data to compare
025             individual models, additional baselines, and ensembles. Different models lead
026             on different datasets, motivating their combination. Compared with selecting
027             the lowest error individual model, the fitted ensemble improves accuracy on 16
028
                datasets and reduces geometric mean relative L2 error by 11.6% when averaged
                across PDE classes and 7.5% when averaged across all datasets.

051   1   I NTRODUCTION
052
053   Scientific design requires solution fields for many choices of physical parameters. Changing a
      boundary condition or diffusivity changes the temperature throughout a domain. A surrogate model


                                                                  1
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2026




054
      learns this map without repeating fine simulations. Multifidelity learning adds cheaper coarse
055   solutions (Kennedy & O’Hagan, 2000, Howard et al., 2023, Niu et al., 2024), leaving a practical
056   choice: which model should use them? Transfer learning, joint training, and coarse field correction
057   use different fidelity relationships, and their accuracy can change as those relationships weaken
058   (Faza et al., 2026).
059
      We train a surrogate library and use additional known fine solutions to fit ensemble weights.
060
      AUTO MF compares three rules: select the most accurate individual model, average models
061   according to their individual errors, or fit averaging weights to minimize the combined error. New
062   parameters produce a fine field without another coarse solve (Figure 1). Each dataset has its own
063   fitted ensemble, with one weight per model fixed across inputs and grid points. Automation selects
064   and combines models within prescribed training recipes.
065
      An ensemble increases surrogate computation while reusing expensive field data. For climate
066
      modeling, the Regional Aerosol Model Intercomparison Project (RAMIP) reports indicative costs
067   of approximately 100,000 CPU core hours for CESM2.1 and 400,000 for UKESM1 per coupled
068   simulation spanning roughly 36 simulated years (Wilcox et al., 2023, Appendix A). Core hours
069   measure total processor usage. These figures describe RAMIP simulations, not the production cost
070   of our ERA5 archive. Niu et al. (2024, Section 6) similarly describe their extra inference overhead
071   as “small compared with the actual simulators”. When generating fields dominates the budget,
072   additional surrogate computation can therefore be reasonable. AUTO MF reuses available training
073   fields across candidates and the same reserved fine examples across selection and combination rules.
074   This approach builds on engineering surrogate ensembles (Goel et al., 2007, Acar & Rais-Rohani,
075   2009, Viana et al., 2009), automated model libraries (Feurer et al., 2015, Erickson et al., 2020),
076   and comparative studies of multifidelity field surrogates (Brunel et al., 2025). Our contribution
077   has three connected parts. First, we construct a library of nine field surrogates by adapting
078   established architectures to transfer, pooled supervision, coarse field correction, and fine only
079   prediction. A common parameter to field interface makes these different uses of training data
080   available for selection and combination. Second, we assemble a benchmark of 22 datasets from
081
      our simulations and imported data, documenting their fidelity definitions and comparing models
      on common evaluation cases within each dataset. Third, AUTO MF connects this library and
082
      benchmark through an automated ensemble workflow with an explicitly counted budget of additional
083
      fine solutions. The underlying architectural components and ensemble weighting principles are
084   established.
085
086   We first compare individual library models with additional baselines, then ask whether ensemble
087
      fitting improves on choosing one model. Error analysis, fitting loss controls, and ERA5 climate
      emulation (Hersbach et al., 2020, Niu et al., 2024) examine when combining predictions helps. This
088
      separates library accuracy from reliable selection. Historical results informed development, so these
089
      comparisons remain retrospective.

092   2   BACKGROUND : FROM SURROGATE SELECTION TO FIELD ENSEMBLES
093
094
      Engineering studies already show why choosing one surrogate can be difficult and why combining
095
      several can help. Goel et al. (2007) weight surrogates using error estimates, Acar & Rais-Rohani
096   (2009) and Strömberg (2021) optimize metamodel weights, and Viana et al. (2009) compare cross
097   validation selection, weighted prediction, and library subsets. For functional outputs, Brunel et al.
098   (2025) compare more than a dozen multifidelity surrogates in a common framework. Their findings
099   connect model performance to fidelity correlation, training size, and residual complexity. These
100   studies establish both the ensemble principle and the need for comparisons across problems. We
101   extend this empirical line of work to a surrogate library for fields and measure combination gains
102   under small, explicitly counted fitting budgets.
103   The weighting rules follow stacked generalization (Wolpert, 1992), stacked regression (Breiman,
104   1996), and Super Learner (van der Laan et al., 2007). Greedy ensemble selection (Caruana et al.,
105   2004), auto sklearn (Feurer et al., 2015, 2022), and AutoGluon (Erickson et al., 2020) automate
106   selection from broader libraries. Related multifidelity workflows automate other parts of the process:
107   EL MFS combines low fidelity models inside hierarchical fusion (Wang et al., 2024), AQBMF
      screens low fidelity information and surrogate candidates (Lee et al., 2026), and the MAESTRO


                                                        2
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2026




108
      preprint screens families by leave one out validation (Kim et al., 2026). Our ensemble fitting stage
109   acts on completed target field predictions from different fidelity strategies. We evaluate that stage
110   and its library, without a direct comparison against these complete engineering automation systems.
111
112
      The library draws on Fourier neural operators (FNO) (Li et al., 2021), DeepONet (Lu et al., 2021),
      wavelet neural operators (WNO) (Tripura & Chakraborty, 2022), and Transolver (Wu et al., 2024).
113
      Multifidelity operator learning also includes DeepONet (Howard et al., 2023), MF WNO (Thakur
114
      et al., 2022), and FNO for carbon storage (Tang et al., 2023). MFRNP uses decoded coarse outputs
115   in residual prediction (Niu et al., 2024), while FIRE uses coarse predictive distributions (Yu et al.,
116   2026). Appendix B.1 identifies the components we adapt and the departures from their original
117   formulations. HyperNOs addresses automated operator training (Ghiotto, 2026), and LANE SI
118   combines sea ice fields (Borisova & Nikitin, 2024). PDEBench and The Well supply broader
119   physical benchmarks (Takamoto et al., 2022, Ohana et al., 2024). Our study starts from supplied
120   training data and fits ensemble weights, leaving simulation acquisition to approaches such as active
121   multi resolution learning (Li et al., 2024).

144   3.2   S URROGATE MODEL LIBRARY
145
146   The surrogate library contains nine models, M1 to M9 in Table 2, chosen to vary both the
147
      representation of a field and the way coarse data enter learning. Transfer can reuse features across
      fidelities, joint supervision shares information during training, and correction uses a coarse field as a
148
      spatial reference. A fine only reduced basis model supplies an alternative when coarse information
149
      is less useful. We adapt these strategies to the same parameter input and target field interface,
150   including the internal coarse prediction or retrieval required by correctors. Three models first
151   learn from coarse fields and then finetune on fine fields, following multifidelity transfer learning
152   (Lyu et al., 2023). FiLM FNO transfer combines Fourier mixing (Li et al., 2021) with parameter
153   conditioned featurewise linear modulation (FiLM) (Perez et al., 2018). ConvNeXt transfer uses
154   convolutional blocks in an encoder and decoder with skip connections (Liu et al., 2022). Wavelet
155   transfer uses a custom wavelet representation inspired by WNO (Tripura & Chakraborty, 2022).
156   Together they test coarse initialization across different spatial representations. All pairs FNO instead
157   pools supervision across fidelity pairs before fine training. It takes parameters and two fidelity labels
158
      as inputs. Poseidon motivates pair enumeration (Herde et al., 2024), while our FNO (Li et al., 2021)
      uses neither its temporal operator nor a supplied source field.
159
160   Four models use coarse predictions or retrieved fields to guide a correction. Distribution FNO
161   predicts a residual from coarse ensemble summaries, adapting FIRE’s distribution conditioning idea
      (Yu et al., 2026). Retrieved field DeepONet uses a branch and trunk representation (Lu et al., 2021,


                                                           3
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2026




162
      2022) to correct a neighboring coarse training field. The slice attention and ConvNeXt correctors
163   refine a predicted coarse field through six updates, using modified Transolver inspired attention
164   (Wu et al., 2024) and convolutional blocks (Liu et al., 2022), respectively. Their coarse inputs are
165   assembled out of fold with four ensemble members per case. Finally, POD GP predicts coefficients
166   in a proper orthogonal decomposition (POD) basis using Gaussian processes (GP) (Higdon et al.,
167   2008). It tests whether a compact field representation can be learned directly from fine solutions.
168   Our adaptations specify how parameters condition each model, how fidelities supervise it, and how
169   coarse references are constructed. Appendix B.1 explains for each model the source components,
170   our changes, and the inclusion rationale. Architecture and training cost both vary across the library,
171
      so rankings cannot be attributed to one design choice alone.

245
246   4.1         DATASETS AND COMPARISON COVERAGE
247
248   We assemble a benchmark of 22 datasets spanning elliptic equations, diffusion, convection, shocks,
249   porous flow, reaction diffusion, waves, and ERA5 climate emulation (Hersbach et al., 2020, Niu
250   et al., 2024). Figure 2 shows one fine field from each dataset, illustrating the range of field structures
251   and grid resolutions. The collection combines our simulations with imported parameter and field
252
      data, using different grids or data sources as fidelities. Euler, Burgers, and Shallow water use
      the published PyClaw finite volume framework (Ketcheson et al., 2012). Their problem designs
253
      include Euler quadrant configurations motivated by The Well (Ohana et al., 2024) and a radial
254
      dam break adapted from PDEBench (Takamoto et al., 2022). Custom solvers are supported by
255   targeted analytical and numerical checks, including a Barenblatt solution test for Porous medium
256   and mesh refinement for Allen Cahn (Appendix A.1). Several datasets describe the same governing
257   equation, so the roster represents 22 prediction tasks rather than 22 independent physical families
258   (Appendix A).
259
      We evaluate all nine library models and their ensembles on all 22 datasets. Models are trained using
260
      the training examples available for each dataset, with additional fine examples reserved for ensemble
261   fitting. Reported comparisons use the same evaluation cases within each dataset and fitting partition.
262   Each final surrogate model uses a single seed. Baseline settings, model specific training schedules,
263   fitting and validation allocations, and comparison coverage are detailed in Appendices B and D.1.
264

291   5.1   D IFFERENT MODELS LEAD ON DIFFERENT DATASETS
292
293   The library contains strong individual models, but their relative accuracy depends on the prediction
294   task. Seven of its nine members achieve the lowest error on at least one PDE dataset. Wavelet
295
      transfer leads on Darcy, POD GP on the Poisson variants, and distribution FNO on the heat variants,
      while the ConvNeXt corrector leads on Cavity and Euler. The four additional reaction diffusion tasks
296
      each favor a different library member. This variation motivates keeping several ways of using coarse
297
      information available: choosing one architecture for the entire collection would discard models that
298   are useful on particular problems. Table 1 shows these differences directly, placing each individual
299   model beside the ensemble rules and the strongest additional baseline for each dataset.
300
301
      The best library member outperforms the best available additional baseline on 15 of the 21 PDE
      datasets. Here the best model in each collection is identified after evaluation, so this comparison
302
      measures the accuracy available within the library. The fitted mixture also achieves lower aggregate
303
      error than the best additional baseline, while individual baselines remain preferable on some datasets
304   (Appendix D.2). The next question is whether the reserved fitting examples can turn this available
305   accuracy into a useful prediction rule. Table 2 summarizes the aggregate comparisons and Elo
306   ranking, with individual baseline errors in Appendix D.1.
307
308   5.2   C OMBINING PREDICTIONS OFTEN IMPROVES ACCURACY
309
310   Using the same five fitting examples, the fitted mixture improves on the Selected model on 16 of
311   22 datasets. It reduces geometric mean relative L2 error by 11.6% across PDE classes and 7.5%
312   across all datasets. Both rules make their decisions before observing the evaluation fields, so this
313
      comparison tests how effectively they use the available fine solutions. The advantage over a best
      individual model identified retrospectively from evaluation answers is much smaller and depends on
314
      aggregation (Appendix D.2). The practical benefit is therefore lower error when model choice must
315
      be made before the next fine field is known, with failures as well as improvements visible in Table 1.
316   Figure 3 provides one field example.

      6   A NALYSIS : WHY AVERAGING CAN HELP
422
423
424
      Averaging can improve predictions when spatial errors partly offset. For a fixed library, classical
      ensemble analysis writes the expected squared relative error as
425
426
                          R(w) = E[w⊤ G(x, y)w] = w⊤ Cw,                      C = E[G(x, y)].                    (6)
427
428   Here G(x, y) is the error product matrix for a new input and target pair, E averages over such pairs,
429   C is its population mean, and R(w) is the mixture’s expected squared relative error. Its diagonal
430   measures individual error and its other entries retain alignment and shared bias (Breiman, 1996,
431   Krogh & Vedelsby, 1994). The inverse error mixture can exploit complementary predictions even
      though it uses only individual accuracy to choose weights.


                                                           8
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2026




432
      This identity does not guarantee useful weights from a small fitting set, and the √      bound in
433
      Appendix F.2 does not certify accuracy from five fields. The reported metric L(w) = E[ w⊤ Gw]
434
      averages unsquared relative error and can favor different weights from R even with unlimited fitting
435   data. Appendix F.1 gives a counterexample and a direct fitting control.

438   7   A PPLICATION TO CLIMATE FIELD EMULATION
439
440   ERA5 asks whether the workflow remains useful beyond the simulation datasets. The task maps 12
441
      emissions related inputs to temperature fields, combining MFRNP’s climate assembly (Niu et al.,
      2024) with ERA5 reanalysis (Hersbach et al., 2020), in the climate emulation setting motivated by
442
      ClimateBench (Watson-Parris et al., 2022). It contains 55 fine training inputs, ten fitting inputs, and
443
      seven evaluation inputs. Reserved inputs are excluded from all training fidelities. All nine models
444   predict on a 128 × 256 working grid.
445
446   POD GP is the most accurate of the nine library models on ERA5, with a mean relative L2
447
      error of 6.7369%. With five fitting examples, the inverse error mixture reduces this error to
      5.8146%, a 13.69% relative improvement. The fitted mixture reaches 6.4877%, improving by
448
      3.70%. Both mixtures therefore improve on the best individual model in the library. Table 1 shows
449
      the individual models and mixtures alongside the strongest additional baseline, with all baseline
450   results in Appendix D.1.

453   8   D ISCUSSION AND LIMITATIONS
454
455   The findings connect the diversity of the surrogate library to a practical model choice procedure.
456
      Different representations and uses of coarse data provide accurate predictions on different problems.
      Reserved fine examples then guide how much each model contributes before the next field is known.
457
      Mixtures often improve on selecting one model, while inverse error weighting shows that much
458
      of this gain is accessible through individual error estimates. For an application with expensive
459   simulations, additional surrogate computation can therefore be a useful way to exploit the available
460   fields. Once the library is trained, comparing and fitting the three rules reuses its predictions without
461   retraining the models.
462
      These results do not isolate a benefit specific to multifidelity data. The library includes a fine only
463
      model, many datasets have equally sized coarse and fine training tables, and validation allocations
464
      vary. Establishing data efficiency requires a fine only library and alternative uses of fitting labels
465   under equal total label and computation budgets, counting every candidate and shared coarse
466   ensemble. Whether the additional surrogate computation is worthwhile depends on simulation cost,
467   the number of prediction queries, and the required accuracy. Net cost savings and comparison with
468   complete engineering automation systems remain open.

470
      9   C ONCLUSION
471
472
      The strongest surrogate for multifidelity field prediction changes across problems. AUTO MF
473   combines an adapted library of nine neural and reduced basis models with an ensemble fitting
474   workflow. Reserved fine solutions determine which models to select or combine, and new inputs
475   require no additional coarse simulation. The assembled benchmark compares these choices across
476   field structures and fidelity relationships.
477
      Across 22 datasets, the fitted mixture improves on selecting one model on 16 datasets using five
478
      reserved fine examples. Inverse error weighting captures most of this benefit. ERA5 extends the
479   comparison beyond PDE simulations. These findings support ensemble fitting when uncertain model
480   choice and expensive field data justify additional surrogate computation.

820   A.2    T RAINING , FITTING , AND EVALUATION CASES
821
822   Reserved fitting and evaluation inputs are excluded from LF and HF training before normalization,
823   basis construction, or model fitting. Within each PDE partition, input hashes separate fitting from
824   evaluation and define nested budgets K ∈ {5, 10, 20}. Every rule uses the same fitting allowance,
825   including any selection or tuning. Models remain fixed across three partitions, which measure
826   sensitivity to case assignment rather than variation across training seeds. Some cases change
827
      roles between partitions. Across the 21 PDE datasets, 3,058 query rows produce 1,842 evaluation
      appearances corresponding to 1,505 distinct dataset and row pairs. Shared simulations across
828
      related datasets can further reduce physical independence. Historical evaluation results influenced
829
      development and reporting scope, making these comparisons retrospective.
830
831   Allen Cahn, Cahn Hilliard II, Fisher KPP, and Phase field crystal each supply 400 training inputs
832   per fidelity. Their query pools contain 78, 100, 100, and 100 inputs. Each partition reserves 16
833
      evaluation cases for Allen Cahn and 20 for each of the other three datasets. ERA5 has 55 HF training
      inputs, ten fitting pool inputs, and seven evaluation inputs, with a smallest retained LF table of 313
834
      rows. The additional Heat I inputs in Appendix E.3 form a separate supplementary evaluation. The
835
      subset and loss diagnostics cover the original 17 PDE tasks, while the primary comparison covers
836   all 22 datasets.
837
838   Predictions share target grids, scaling, and input identities within each comparison. Source hashes,
839
      saved target identities, or checkpoint evaluation verify correspondence. Saved weights are fixed
      before evaluation answers are scored, and replay checks reproduce individual errors, mixture errors,
840
      and aggregate denominators. ERA5 answer files are separated from model training, and changing
841
      evaluation answers leaves fitted weights unchanged in the collector check. These checks verify

844   checkpoint provenance records remain in the companion package.

      B     M ODEL ADAPTATIONS AND TRAINING
865
866
      The library varies both the representation of a field and how coarse data enter learning. We first
867   identify the retained published components and our adaptations, then give the training settings. The
868   additional baseline descriptions use the same distinction between a source idea and a complete
869   published method.
870
871   B.1   M ODEL SOURCES , ADAPTATIONS , AND RATIONALE
872
873   Each library member combines a field representation with a training and inference procedure. We
874   describe M1 to M9 individually below, identifying the published components retained, the changes
875   made for parameter to field prediction, and the reason for including each model. Several members
876   combine ideas from more than one paper. The resulting library is an integration of adapted models,
877   not nine independent reproductions or nine newly proposed architectures. All accept parameters
878
      alone at deployment. Appendix B.2 gives their training budgets and data use. The rationales below
      motivate the library’s composition, while the experiments assess its predictions. They do not isolate
879
      the effect of every architectural change.
880
881
      M1: FiLM FNO transfer. The starting components are FNO’s learned Fourier mixing (Li et al.,
882
      2021), FiLM’s featurewise affine conditioning (Perez et al., 2018), and the LF pretraining followed
883   by HF finetuning strategy of Lyu et al. (2023). We retain spectral convolutions with local residual
884   paths and adapt the input interface to a simulation parameter vector. The spatial input contains
885   coordinates only. At each block, a parameter network generates a scale and shift applied after group
886   normalization, so the parameters influence features throughout the network. Related parameter
887   conditioning for PDE emulation is studied by Shokar et al. (2025). Our model differs from our
888   parameter concatenation FNO references, which supply parameters as constant spatial channels at
889   the input. The same weights are first trained on LF fields and then finetuned on HF fields. This
890   combination tests whether repeated parameter conditioning and a coarse initialization provide an
891
      effective way to generate globally coupled fields.
892
893
      M2: All pairs FNO. M2 retains M1’s FNO and FiLM components (Li et al., 2021, Perez et al.,
      2018) and adapts the idea of pair enumeration from Poseidon (Herde et al., 2024). Poseidon uses
894
      pairs of states for temporal operator learning. Here the pairs index source and target fidelities.
895
      We append their normalized labels to the parameter vector, pool the corresponding target fields on
896   a common working grid, and train one conditional FNO before HF finetuning. The source label
897   specifies a training pair, but no source field enters the network. Poseidon’s architecture, pretrained
898   weights, and temporal operator are not used. For ERA5, exact input joins retain each target field
899   with its own parameters. The purpose is to let one model share information across fidelity tasks
900   before specializing to the fine field. Pair enumeration also repeats targets and changes optimization
901   cost, so its effect cannot be attributed to fidelity conditioning alone.
902
903   M3: ConvNeXt transfer. ConvNeXt supplies the depthwise spatial convolution, channel
904   expansion, and residual block structure (Liu et al., 2022). We place these blocks in an U shaped
905   encoder and decoder with skip connections (Ronneberger et al., 2015) and a dense field output,
906
      adapting the image classification backbone to field generation. The model starts from coordinates
      and replaces the usual block normalization with group normalization and FiLM conditioning on the
907
      simulation parameters (Perez et al., 2018). Three downsampling stages and a bottleneck process
908
      the field at several resolutions, with corresponding decoder stages restoring the target grid. LF
909   pretraining and HF finetuning follow the transfer principle of Lyu et al. (2023). The rationale is to
910   provide local spatial processing alongside M1’s Fourier mixing, while retaining the same type of
911   parameter conditioning and transfer schedule.
912
913   M4: Distribution FNO. FIRE conditions a fine correction on summaries of a coarse predictive
914   distribution using tabular foundation models and in context inference (Yu et al., 2026). M4 retains
915   the distribution conditioning idea and replaces those models with FNOs trained on spatial fields (Li
916   et al., 2021). Five LF members predict a coarse field at the query parameters. Their pointwise mean,
917   standard deviation, and 10th, 50th, and 90th percentiles become spatial input channels for a residual
      FNO, together with coordinates and broadcast parameters. The residual is added to the coarse mean


                                                       17
      Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2026




918
      to predict the fine field. Both stages are trained by gradient optimization, so this implementation
919   does not retain FIRE’s inference without model retraining. The ensemble summaries are features of
920   prediction variability, not a guarantee of accurate uncertainty estimates. The rationale is to expose
921   coarse disagreement as well as the mean to the correction model. This recipe has no out of fold
922   construction and is distinct from the coarse ensembles used by M8 and M9.
923
924   M5: Wavelet transfer. WNO motivates learning in wavelet coordinates (Tripura & Chakraborty,
925   2022). We replace M1’s Fourier mixing with a custom single level db4 wavelet transform, while
926   retaining its coordinate input, FiLM conditioning (Perez et al., 2018), local residual paths, and
927   LF to HF transfer schedule (Lyu et al., 2023). Within each wavelet subband, learned weights
928   mix channels at coefficient positions inside a capped leading block. Other positions pass through
929   unchanged before the inverse transform. These indices describe positions within a subband, not a
930
      Fourier frequency cutoff. The implementation also handles rectangular grids, odd grid sizes, and
      one dimensional grids. It is a custom wavelet representation rather than an exact reproduction of
931
      the published WNO. Its inclusion tests whether spatially localized wavelet features provide useful
932
      predictions alongside the spectral and convolutional transfer models.
933
934
      M6: Retrieved field DeepONet. DeepONet supplies the branch and trunk representation (Lu
935
      et al., 2021), while the multifidelity use of a coarse prediction as a reference is motivated by
936   Lu et al. (2022). We use a Cartesian product DeepONet whose branch receives the parameters
937   concatenated with a stored LF training field and whose trunk receives target grid coordinates. A
938   nearest neighbour search in standardized parameter space selects that field. The network learns a
939   residual relative to its interpolated version, which is then added back to form the fine prediction.
940   Retrieval is the deployment adaptation: a new query requires parameters only and no new coarse
941   solve. The rationale is to exploit a nearby physical field without first learning a separate coarse
942   surrogate. If an exact LF match exists at a training input, retrieval supplies that match, whereas an
943   unseen query uses a neighboring input. This difference in reference quality remains a limitation.
944
      The implemented residual model is also distinct from the composite multifidelity architecture of
      Howard et al. (2023).
945
946
      M7: POD GP. Basis emulation represents a large field through a small set of coefficients (Higdon
947
      et al., 2008). M7 retains this principle, constructing a POD basis by singular value decomposition
948
      of centered HF training fields. A Gaussian process maps parameters to each retained coefficient,
949   and the predicted coefficients are projected back through the basis and added to the training mean
950   field. Our implementation uses an RBF kernel with automatic relevance determination and shares
951   covariance hyperparameters across coefficient outputs. It does not implement the full Bayesian
952   hierarchy of the cited model. M7 is deliberately fine only: no coarse field enters basis construction,
953   fitting, or inference. It supplies a compact alternative when the field varies mainly in a few directions
954   or when learning a coarse correction is less effective than learning the fine coefficients directly.
955
956   M8: Slice attention corrector. Transolver motivates grouping spatial cells into learned slice
957   tokens (Wu et al., 2024). We retain pooled spatial summaries but change the attention operation.
958   Original Transolver normalizes each cell’s assignments across slices, applies attention between
959   slice tokens, and maps back using the assignments. Our pooling weights instead normalize across
960
      cells, and cell queries attend directly to the pooled slice tokens. The model receives the current
      field, coordinates with sinusoidal features, and FiLM conditioning on the parameters (Perez et al.,
961
      2018). A coarse surrogate ensemble supplies its initial state, with out of fold predictions for training
962
      cases. A head initialized to zero predicts additive corrections through six damped updates with
963   shared weights. Training penalizes spatial squared error and Fourier magnitude discrepancy at each
964   update, plus a nonzero update when started at the target field. This encourages an equilibrium
965   without guaranteeing contraction. The adaptation tests whether pooled global interactions can refine
966   a predicted coarse field using parameters alone at deployment. Its attention and iterative recipe differ
967   from the published Transolver.
968
969   M9: ConvNeXt corrector. M9 retains ConvNeXt’s spatial and channel mixing blocks (Liu et al.,
970   2022) and adapts them to the same correction task as M8. It uses a custom U shaped encoder
971   and decoder with skip connections (Ronneberger et al., 2015), three downsampling stages and a
      bottleneck, and FiLM parameter conditioning (Perez et al., 2018). Unlike M3, which generates a


                                                         18
       Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2026




972
       field from coordinates and parameters after transfer training, M9 receives the current coarse estimate
973    as a spatial input and outputs an additive update. Its head is initialized to zero, making the initial
974    refinement the identity. The coarse ensemble construction, six damped updates, shared weights,
975    and spatial, spectral, and target state penalties follow the M8 recipe. The rationale is to offer local
976    processing at several resolutions as an alternative to pooled attention when correcting the same kind
977    of coarse prediction. This is a ConvNeXt based iterative corrector, not the original classification
978    model.
979    The additional baseline adaptations below use the same distinction between a source idea and a
980    complete published method. In particular, B1, B5, and B8 omit central probabilistic components
981    of co kriging, DMFAL, and IFC. Their scores describe the implemented references, not faithful
982    reproductions of those complete methods. Common evaluation cases make the scores comparable
983    while leaving architecture and resource effects coupled.
984
985    B1, B2, and B11: classical multifidelity field references. B1 applies an autoregressive
986    discrepancy idea (Kennedy & O’Hagan, 2000) to POD GP field models. It fits one scalar coupling
987    coefficient in field space, constructs a separate POD GP for residuals, and combines posterior means
988    at deployment. This is a plug in approximation rather than joint probabilistic co kriging. B2
989
       introduces nonlinear dependence on LF coefficients (Perdikaris et al., 2017). Its HF coefficient
       kernel combines input dependent coupling, an LF coefficient kernel, and a discrepancy kernel.
990
       It shares covariance hyperparameters across HF outputs and propagates marginal LF uncertainty
991
       through Monte Carlo samples. B11 is a cheaper affine alternative inspired by multifidelity surrogate
992    work (Zhang et al., 2018). One slope and one intercept are fitted jointly over HF training cases
993    and pixels, using observed LF fields when paired and emulated LF fields otherwise. They are then
994    applied to the LF POD GP prediction. It does not fit an affine mapping separately for each mode.
995    Paired training rows can use observed LF fields, whereas deployment uses predicted LF fields.
996
997    B3, B4, and B5: neural fusion references. B3 uses the composite correlation idea of Meng &
998    Karniadakis (2020). A tanh MLP maps parameters and coordinates to an LF value. This stage is
999    frozen before linear and nonlinear HF networks are trained on the original inputs and LF prediction,
1000   with weight decay on the nonlinear branch. B4 follows the composite DeepONet idea (Howard et al.,
1001   2023), predicting fixed coarse sensors with an LF DeepONet and feeding parameters plus sensors to
1002
       linear and nonlinear HF DeepONets. The LF stage is frozen and the HF branches are trained jointly
       in a subsequent stage. B5 takes hierarchical latent transfer as inspiration from DMFAL (Li et al.,
1003
       2022a). It uses two encoders and linear field decoders, with the second encoder receiving parameters
1004
       and the first latent vector. Joint LF and HF squared losses replace the probabilistic objective. Both
1005   the variational last layer and active acquisition are omitted. The name latent MF network reflects
1006   these substantial departures.
1007
1008   B6 and B7: alternative decoder and probabilistic models. B6 uses NOMAD’s nonlinear
1009   decoding principle (Seidman et al., 2022). A parameter MLP produces a latent vector and
1010   a coordinate conditioned MLP decodes the field. The model is implemented independently,
1011   with multifidelity learning supplied by LF pretraining and HF finetuning. B7 directly imports
1012   MFRNP’s upstream MultiFidelityModel (Niu et al., 2024). Its adapter changes data loading,
1013   normalization, resizing, and training. A scalar mean and standard deviation from the first fidelity
1014   normalize inputs, while outputs are scaled per fidelity. The longest grid side is capped at 256,
1015
       rectangular grids are supported, and bilinear antialiased resizing replaces the upstream transform.
       Batch size is limited by the smallest fidelity, with independent shuffling across fidelities. When
1016
       HF training has at most ten examples, all rows are retained and the context fraction range changes
1017
       from [0.20, 0.25] to [0.40, 0.60]. Hidden width is 128, latent width is 64, and there are three hidden
1018   layers. Adam uses learning rate and epsilon of 10−3 . HF likelihood receives weight two and LF
1019   likelihood weight one. Latent summaries are saved from the final training batch. These are protocol
1020   adaptations of an upstream model, rather than a reproduction of its published experiment.
1021
1022   B8, B9, and B10: fidelity and transfer FNO references. B8 forms latent spatial channels with
1023   an FNO and mixes them with weights predicted from [ℓ, ℓ2 ], the fidelity label and its square. These
1024   two features condition the spatial basis weights on fidelity. It uses maximum absolute scaling per
1025   fidelity, the nearest registered scaler for an intermediate fidelity, LF warm up, and joint LF and HF
       finetuning. IFC motivates fidelity dependent representation (Li et al., 2022b), but its neural ODE


                                                         19
       Under review as a conference paper at ICLR 2026




1026
       and Gaussian process are absent. The descriptive name fidelity basis FNO makes this distinction
1027   explicit. B9 and B10 are two archived entries for parameter concatenation FNO transfer (Lyu et al.,
1028   2023). In the inspected release, their model files are byte identical and their training scripts differ
1029   only in repository paths and reported identifiers. The labels FNO transfer I and II distinguish saved
1030   result entries, not independent baseline families. Their different saved scores remain unexplained
1031   by the inspected source. Both are retained to make the archived comparison auditable, but neither
1032   their number nor their score difference is evidence for architectural diversity or a reproducible recipe
1033   difference. For ERA5 and the four additional reaction diffusion datasets, the identical recipe is fitted
1034   once and its predictions are explicitly reused for B10, with no additional training cost.

1036   B.2    T RAINING SETTINGS AND DATA USE
1037

       C.1    N UMERICAL FITTING
1083
1084   The Selected model, Inverse error mixture, and Fitted mixture use the same K input and fine
1085   field pairs. Their definitions appear in Section 3. For joint fitting, the empirical error matrix
1086   is symmetrized and divided by max(tr(C)/M, b      10−30 ). Here the trace sums diagonal errors and
1087   M = 9 counts models. This positive scaling changes the objective magnitude without changing
1088   the matrix condition number or the exact minimizer. SLSQP uses the analytic gradient, bounds
1089   0 ≤ wm ≤ 1, the unit sum constraint, tolerance 10−12 , and at most 300 iterations. Uniform weights
1090   and the lowest diagonal singleton initialize separate solves. Finite solutions with unit sum residual
1091
       below 10−6 are clipped to nonnegative values and renormalized. Feasible starting points remain
       candidates, and the lowest fitting objective determines the retained weights. These checks establish
1092
       numerical feasibility, not generalization to new inputs.
1093
1094   Uniform averaging, used as a control in Table 7, assigns every member weight 1/M . Forming the
1095   error matrices costs O(KM 2 P ) for P values per field. Streaming predictions and keeping only
1096   these matrices requires O(KM 2 ) storage. These costs exclude surrogate training and inference.
1097
       The companion package preserves additional regularization, pair fitting, and coarse input refresh
       experiments separately from the three main rules.

           Algorithm 1: train models, fit the ensemble, and predict
1102       Input: LF/HF training tables, K reserved fine cases, fixed model recipes, and one prescribed rule:

       G.1   R EPRODUCIBILITY STATEMENT
1515
1516   We will release the benchmark datasets and the code used to train and evaluate all baseline
1517   implementations, the nine surrogate models, and the ensemble methods, together with data
1518   preparation scripts, fixed splits, experiment configurations, and scripts for reproducing the reported
1519   tables and figures.
1520   We retain the datasets, training and simulation code, data preparation routines, and experiment
1521   records for all 22 datasets in the benchmark and experiment archives. The manuscript package
1522   supplies numerical snapshots, plotting and table scripts, a workbook, and a portable program for
1523   fitting ensembles from saved predictions and combining new predictions without their evaluation
1524   answers. Input identities, source hashes, saved weights, and replay checks connect these artifacts to
1525   the reported scores. The verified clean rebuild covers ensemble fitting, saved prediction evaluation,
1526   tables, and figures. It does not retrain the library or rerun the simulation solvers. The companion
1527
       supplement indexes enlarged dataset panels, exploratory experiments, and detailed audit records
       omitted from the condensed appendix.
