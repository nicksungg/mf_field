0 · The problem, in plain terms

Engineers and scientists constantly need to know how a physical quantity is distributed over space: the temperature at every point of a heated plate, the pressure at every point around a wing, the humidity at every point of the atmosphere. Such a "value at every point" object is called a field — for us, concretely, an image (a 2-D grid of numbers, e.g. 128×128).

Physics tells you which field is the right one through a partial differential equation (PDE) — an equation that constrains how the field bends and flows locally (you may have seen the heat equation in a calculus class; that is exactly the kind of thing meant here). You almost never solve a PDE with pen and paper. Instead, a numerical solver chops space into a grid and grinds out the field cell by cell. And here is the economic fact the whole project rests on: the finer the grid, the more accurate the field — and the much more expensive the solve. Halving the cell size in both directions multiplies the work by roughly 4–8×. A coarse 32×32 solve might take seconds; a fine 256×256 solve, hours.

"Fidelity" just means "how trustworthy the simulation is." Low-fidelity (LF) = the cheap draft: coarse grid, loose tolerances, big timesteps. High-fidelity (HF) = the expensive final: fine grid, tight tolerances. An LF field of the same scenario looks like a blurry, slightly distorted version of the HF field — right about the broad strokes, wrong about fine detail, and occasionally wrong about where a feature sits. Some datasets have a whole ladder of fidelities (e.g. grids of 8×8, 16×16, 32×32, then the HF grid), not just two rungs.

Multi-fidelity field prediction (MFFP) is then: given lots of cheap LF fields and very few expensive HF fields, train a model that predicts the HF field for a brand-new scenario X. The scientific question behind the whole model zoo: what is the best way to make the abundant cheap data compensate for the scarcity of the expensive data? Each of the 30 families is one answer to that question.

1 · The same problem, in ML terms you already know

Strip the physics away and this is supervised regression where the output is an image. The table maps it onto a project you might have done in class:

Why not just train a big network on the HF pairs and call it a day? Because with tens of examples, a network with millions of parameters memorizes instead of generalizing — the classic small-data failure. And why not train on the LF pairs? Because then you learn to predict the blurry draft, not the sharp answer. Every family below is a different way to have it both ways.

One benchmark detail worth knowing up front: the zoo is scored on 17 datasets (heat flow, Poisson problems, weather fields, …), and models are ranked two ways — by Elo (the chess rating, here computed from per-dataset pairwise wins: beating many models on many datasets → high Elo, robust to one catastrophic dataset) and by median rel-L2 (typical error size). The two can disagree, and the leaderboard section explains how to read that.

3 · The FNO backbone, from scratch — read once, reused by 24 of 30 families

Almost every family uses the same network — a Fourier Neural Operator (FNO) — and differs only in how it brings the LF data in. So this section is the one real investment on this page. The backbone's defaults everywhere: 64 hidden channels, 4 blocks, 12 Fourier modes per axis, working grid = the HF grid capped at 256 px. Understand this once and every family below reduces to "same trunk, different fusion".

Fourier in 90 seconds

Any image can be written as a sum of 2-D sine waves of different frequencies — this decomposition is the Fourier transform, and the FFT computes it fast. Low frequencies = broad strokes (the overall warm/cool layout of a temperature field); high frequencies = fine detail and sharp edges.

You have met this idea before even if the name is new: JPEG compression keeps mostly low frequencies and throws away high ones, which is why an over-compressed photo looks smooth and slightly smeared. Hold onto that image — it is exactly what an FNO does to a field, and it explains both the FNO's strength (smooth fields compress beautifully into a few frequencies) and its one structural weakness (sharp edges need many frequencies, and the FNO throws most of them away).

From CNN to FNO in three steps

1. A normal conv layer mixes each pixel with a small neighborhood (3×3). Information crosses the image only after many layers — slow, and resolution-dependent.

2. Math fact (the convolution theorem): a convolution with a filter as big as the whole image is just a per-frequency multiplication in Fourier space. So: FFT the features, multiply each frequency by a learned weight, inverse FFT. One layer = every pixel talks to every pixel.

3. To keep the parameter count finite, learn weights only for the lowest 12×12 frequencies and zero the rest. That single truncation is the FNO's defining trade-off. A bonus of working in frequency space: the same weights work at any grid resolution, which is why FNOs are called neural operators — they learn a map between functions, not between fixed-size pixel arrays.

Preview — sharp datasets (applies to every FNO family): blocks 4–5 are the whole story. A shock wave (a near-discontinuous jump) needs high frequencies that decay only slowly (like 1/k), so deleting everything above mode 12 puts a hard floor of roughly 3–10% rel-L2 on such fields no matter how long you train; and the 1×1 bypass can pass sharp content through but cannot reposition it. Fixes on the drawing board: more modes (factorized to stay affordable), a local convolution path, or a different basis — see the companion report, Part 4.
Fine print (code audit): all image resizing in the shared data loaders is bilinear with antialias=False — LF fields upsampled to the working grid arrive pre-blurred, and very large grids (era5's 721×1440 → 128×256) are downsampled with aliasing artifacts. The training loss is plain MSE in scaler-normalized space in every FNO family (the Transolver/v9 families add a rel-L2 term).

4 · Transfer-learning FNO — the current winners (#1, #2, #8, #10)

If you know intro ML, think: pretrain on ImageNet, fine-tune on your small dataset — literally that recipe. The twist that makes it work unusually well here: the "ImageNet" (the LF pool) is not just similar to the target task, it is the same physics, solved sloppily. So pretraining teaches almost everything, and the scarce HF examples only fine-tune the details.

Multi-fidelity lives in the training schedule, not the architecture: one FNO, trained twice. Stage 1 fits the abundant LF fields (learning rate 1e-3, full epoch budget); stage 2 re-fits all weights on the scarce HF fields (fresh optimizer, lower learning rate 3e-4 so the good LF solution is nudged rather than destroyed, full epoch budget). The LF stage is scaffolding: it moves the weights into a region where "solutions of this PDE family" are easy to represent, so the few HF samples don't have to teach the network from scratch.

The ramp keeps the physics term from fighting the data fit early on.Best median error in the zoo (0.0122). Known physics adds a real but modest increment on the datasets where it applies.

FiLM in depth — channels vs blocks, and what γ / β actually do added after your annotation

If you know intro ML, think: batch-norm has a learnable scale and shift per channel, fixed after training. FiLM is that same pair of knobs — except a small side network predicts them from X, fresh for every sample. (The same trick powers conditional GANs and style transfer, where the "style" vector steers the normalization.)

First, channels vs blocks — the two axes of the network. A block is a stage in depth: this FNO is 4 blocks in a row, and data flows through block 1 → 2 → 3 → 4 like stations on an assembly line, each station doing one round of "mix globally, adjust pointwise, normalize, apply nonlinearity". A channel is a slot of width: between stations, the data is a stack of 64 images of shape H×W — written (batch, 64, H, W). Like RGB, except the network invents its own 64 "colors": after training, one channel might carry "how boundary-layer-ish is this region", another "local curvature", and so on. So the network is 64 channels wide and 4 blocks deep, and those are independent choices.

What FiLM computes, concretely. At every block, a tiny MLP reads X (the ~3 PDE parameters) and outputs 128 numbers: one scale γ_c and one shift β_c for each of the 64 channels. Right after the block's normalization, channel c's entire image is transformed as z_c → γ_c(X) · z_c + β_c(X) — the same γ_c and β_c at every pixel of that channel. That is the meaning of "feature-wise": it modulates whole feature maps, never individual pixels.

What γ and β do, intuitively. Picture a 64-track mixing desk, one track per channel. γ is each track's volume fader: γ_c ≈ 2 means "for this PDE setting, feature c matters — turn it up"; γ_c ≈ 0 mutes it; negative flips its sign. β is each track's baseline trim, and it matters because GELU comes right after: a positive β pushes the channel up into GELU's pass-through zone (the feature flows onward), a negative β pushes it into the dead zone (the feature is suppressed). So γ sets how much of each learned feature to use, β sets whether it clears the nonlinearity at all — and since every block has its own FiLM MLP, the mix gets re-tuned four times on the way through.

Why this wins over concat (the whispers-vs-re-whispers picture from the table above, mechanically): concat hands X to the network once, as 3 extra input images, and can only ever act additively — more input for block 1 to chew on. FiLM gives X a direct multiplicative handle on all 64 features at all 4 depths — a shorter path from parameters to behavior, and a more expressive kind of control (steering the network vs feeding it). The MLPs are zero-initialized (γ starts at exactly 1, β at 0), so on day one the network is precisely the unconditioned FNO and the modulation grows only as training finds it useful — the same training-wheels pattern used by every gate and correction head in the zoo. Cost: ~4.3k extra parameters per block, a rounding error on 4.7M — and it moved this family from #8 to #1.

Preview — sharp datasets: at test time these models never see the LF field, so the ~10 numbers in X alone must pin down exactly where a shock or interface sits. The 12-mode ceiling and MSE's preference for blur apply in full; expect degradation, but likely still best-in-zoo among FNO families. Notably, the physics-residual loss is the zoo's only supervision signal that is exact about position — but it is currently wired up only for the two Poisson datasets, so it is inactive on the new ones unless extended.
Fine print (code audit): the name "FiLM fine-tune" describes the conditioning architecture, not the fine-tuning scope — stage 2 trains all parameters, not just the FiLM layers. Both in-checkout transfer families save their checkpoint only when a stage completes, so a mid-run cluster preemption restarts that stage from epoch 0.

5 · FIRE — "correct the draft, and tell the corrector where the draft is shaky" (#3–#15, #24)

If you know intro ML, think: two ideas you have seen, composed. (1) Boosting: fit a second model to the first model's errors — here, a "residual" network predicts the correction δ = HF − LF-prediction instead of the whole field. (2) Ensembles for uncertainty: train several copies of a model with different random seeds; where their predictions agree, be confident; where they disagree, don't. FIRE's twist is to compute that disagreement per pixel, turn it into extra input images (an uncertainty map), and hand those to the correction network — so the corrector knows where to work hardest.

This is the zoo's novel contribution (a field-data analog of the FIRE tabular method, arXiv:2601.22371). The core bet: where the LF model is uncertain is where the LF→HF gap is large. So instead of feeding the correction network only the LF model's best guess (its mean μ), feed it the LF model's whole predictive distribution, summarized as five per-pixel images: the mean μ, the spread σ, and three quantiles (the 10th/50th/90th-percentile value each pixel might take). Eleven variants share this exact pipeline and differ in one block only: how the LF distribution is obtained — making the family a controlled ablation of uncertainty methods.

Why does FIRE (#3) still trail plain transfer (#1) — and were ResNets involved?

No ResNets anywhere. Every FIRE variant is built from the exact same section-3 FNO backbone as the transfer family — the ablation holds the backbone fixed on purpose, so ranking differences come from the fusion mechanism, not the architecture. The word "residual" here means predicting the residual field δ = HF − μ (the boosting sense: fit a model to another model's leftover error), not ResNet-style skip connections. The closest thing to a skip connection in the zoo is the backbone's own 1×1 bypass (block ⑤).

Given the same backbone, three things explain the gap on this benchmark:

  1. Where the LF knowledge ends up. Transfer bakes the thousands of LF examples into every weight of the final network via pretraining. FIRE's correction network never gets that: it trains from scratch on only the scarce HF pairs, and its LF knowledge arrives only through 5 input channels. Millions of LF-shaped parameters versus five LF-derived input images — on a smooth benchmark, the pretrained weights are simply the stronger way to absorb the cheap data.
  2. The anchor's flaws are inherited. FIRE's prediction is μ + δ, and μ is itself an imperfect neural prediction — blurred, occasionally mispositioning features. The correction δ must spend its capacity undoing μ's mistakes (through the 12-mode bottleneck). Transfer predicts the field directly and has no anchor to apologize for.
  3. This benchmark favors route 1. On these mostly-smooth datasets, X pins down the field well, so predicting directly from X (transfer) loses little by ignoring the per-sample LF field — while FIRE pays LF's blur-and-noise costs regardless.

Worth keeping in perspective: #3 makes FIRE the best of the entire "LF as input" route (section 2) — the gap to #1 is a route-1-vs-route-2 story on FNO-friendly data, not a failure of the uncertainty idea. On sharp datasets, where X alone cannot pin a shock's position and per-sample LF information should matter more, the report expects this comparison to tighten or flip.

Preview — sharp datasets: two structural worries. First, the anchor μ carries the LF solver's mispositioned shock, and the correction δ must erase-and-redraw it through a 12-mode bottleneck. Second, FIRE's hedge — σ lighting up where the shock might be — only works if ensemble members actually disagree there; if the coarse solver makes the same systematic error every time (coarse shock solvers smear shocks the same way in every run), all members inherit it, and σ stays quiet exactly where help is needed. The bell-curve variants (mdn/laplace/dkl) additionally cannot represent "either here or there" position ambiguity at all.
Fine print (code audit): the 9 variants that use the shared training runner ignore --ckpt_dir entirely — no resume after a cluster preemption, a contract violation (only distcond/mcdropout checkpoint, and only at the end of a run). Two names oversell: "batchens" is a multi-head ensemble, not the rank-1 BatchEnsemble of the paper; "cqr" is split-conformal σ-scaling, not textbook conformalized quantile regression. The shared loop also draws a fresh random permutation every mini-batch, so samples can repeat or be skipped within an "epoch".

6 · Coregionalization — one network for all fidelities, with a quality knob (#13, #19–#21)

If you know intro ML, think: multi-task learning with a task-ID input. One network is trained to produce the field at every fidelity level, and an extra scalar input m ∈ [0,1] tells it which quality level you want (0 = coarsest draft, 1 = HF). Training on all rungs jointly forces the network to learn what the rungs share; at test time you simply ask for m = 1. (The odd name comes from the Gaussian-process literature this idea originated in; nothing more is meant by it.)

IFC-style (Li 2022): the LF data enters as extra supervision at other m values — at test time only X and m=1 are consumed (so like transfer, no LF field is consulted for the specific test scenario). The three in-checkout variants differ in where the knob m acts on the network — and the result echoes the transfer family's FiLM lesson almost exactly.

7 · Discrepancy & multi-level correction — the textbook recipes, FNO-ized (#17, #18, #22, #25)

8 · Attention / Transolver — the only families that read the real LF field at test time (#23, #26, #30)

If you know intro ML, think: transformers. Just as a ViT chops an image into patch tokens, these models turn each field into a set of tokens (one per sampled point: its coordinates + values), and fidelities exchange information by cross-attention — each HF query point gets to "look at" a compressed summary of the LF point cloud and pull in what is relevant. No Fourier transform anywhere, so none of the FNO's frequency truncation — but attention over thousands of points is expensive, so everything is squeezed through a small number of learned slice tokens (soft groupings of points, ~32 of them), which is a different bottleneck in disguise. And like all transformers, they are data-hungry — a problem when HF data is the scarce thing.

How to read the two scores

Elo is the chess rating applied to models: every pair of models is compared on every dataset, each comparison is a "game", and ratings rise with wins. It rewards beating many models on many datasets and is robust to one catastrophic blow-up. med relL2 is the median across datasets of the relative error — "on a typical dataset, how far off is this model, as a fraction of the true field's size?" (0.0154 ≈ 1.5% off).

They can disagree, and both disagreements below are informative: a model can have excellent typical error but few outright wins (fno_coreg_residual: median 0.0251 but Elo #21), or broad wins without the best typical error (the FIRE models). For the FIRE families specifically, the mean relL2 is wrecked by a few blow-up datasets — trust Elo and the median.

The four takeaways the numbers support

  1. How you condition beats how much architecture you add — swapping broadcast-concat for FiLM, alone, is #8 → #1.
  2. Known physics adds a little more — the PINN loss gives the best median error (0.0122).
  3. LF uncertainty is a real signal — the FIRE family clusters at #3–#15; but cheap uncertainty (MC-dropout, #24) is worse than no uncertainty at all (plain additive, #18).
  4. Classic correction rules and external methods trail on these (smooth) datasets.

Caveat: this ranking was measured on FNO-friendly data

Nearly all 17 datasets have smooth fields — exactly where a 12-mode spectral method shines. The companion report (MF_Sharp_HighFreq_Report.pdf) argues from theory, external benchmarks, and the code audit that the ranking should roughly hold on the smoother upcoming dataset (Kuramoto–Sivashinsky) but invert on the shock and interface datasets (Euler, Cahn–Hilliard), and proposes nine new families built for that regime (local convolution paths, warp-then-correct registration, band-splitting, level-set heads…).

12 · Glossary — every recurring term, defined once

The problem

Field. A quantity defined at every point in space — here always stored as a 2-D image of numbers (temperature map, pressure map…).

PDE (partial differential equation). The physics law a field must satisfy, phrased as constraints on its local slopes and curvatures. Solved numerically on grids, never by hand.

Fidelity / LF / HF. How trustworthy a simulation is. Low fidelity = coarse grid / loose tolerances: cheap, blurry, plentiful. High fidelity = fine grid: expensive, sharp, scarce. Some datasets have a whole ladder of rungs, not just two.

Fidelity index m ∈ [0,1]. A dataset-assigned label for each rung (0 = coarsest, 1 = HF). Correlated with grid resolution but not computed from it — the values (e.g. {0, 0.143, 0.429, 1}) come with the dataset, and fidelity can also mean solver tolerance or timestep. Networks consume it via small MLPs on [m, m²].

Condition vector X. The ~3–10 numbers describing one scenario (PDE coefficients, boundary values…). The only test-time input every family receives.

rel-L2 / nRMSE. Flatten prediction and truth to vectors; error = ‖pred − truth‖ ⁄ ‖truth‖. 0.01 = "1% off". nRMSE is a normalized-RMSE cousin of the same idea; both are computed per sample and aggregated.

Elo. The chess rating, computed from per-dataset pairwise model-vs-model wins. Robust to a single catastrophic dataset; the benchmark's primary ranking.

composite_nRMSE. The factory's optimization target: the geometric mean over datasets of the best-model-per-dataset nRMSE (geometric so that no one dataset's scale dominates).

The machinery

FNO / SpectralConv2d. A network whose core layer works in frequency space: FFT → keep the lowest 12×12 frequency modes (16 or 20 in two families) → multiply by learned complex weights → inverse FFT. Global mixing in one layer, resolution-independent weights — and a built-in low-pass filter that caps sharpness.

Mode. One 2-D sine-wave frequency in the Fourier decomposition. Low modes = broad strokes; high modes = edges and fine detail. "Keeping 12 modes" = keeping only the 12 lowest frequencies per axis.

FiLM. Feature-wise Linear Modulation: a small MLP maps a conditioner (X or m) to a per-channel scale γ and shift β applied after normalization in every block — batch-norm's knobs, driven by a side network. Always zero-initialized here (γ=1, β=0) so training starts from the unconditioned network.

Residual / δ. A correction added to a baseline: pred = baseline + δ. Learning the (smaller) gap instead of the whole field — boosting's move.

Ensemble / σ. Several independently trained copies of a model; their per-pixel disagreement (standard deviation σ) is an uncertainty map. Quantiles q10/q50/q90 = the values a pixel lands under 10%/50%/90% of the time.

Zero-initialization. Starting a new component at exactly zero effect, so the model begins as its simpler baseline and the component grows only if training finds it useful. Used by every FiLM, gate, and correction head in the zoo.

Per-fidelity scaler. scaler[m] = max|Y_train at m|; training happens on Y/scaler, predictions are re-multiplied. Fixes ~40× magnitude gaps between rungs.

Working grid. The HF native grid, capped at 256 px on the longest side; everything is bilinearly resampled onto it.

ifc_raw / npz_l. The two dataset storage formats. ifc_raw test splits are HF-only — which forces LF-consuming families to synthesize test-time LF by downsampling HF (see fine print, section 8).

Smoke vs full. Smoke = the quick per-cycle gate (200 epochs, 2 datasets, ≤30 min on one H100); full = the leaderboard run (2500 epochs × 17 datasets).

13 · The fine print — audit findings, and the ML lessons inside them

Collected from the line-by-line code read (2026-07-02). None of these invalidate the leaderboard; all of them matter for interpreting it. They also happen to be a tour of classic ML-engineering pitfalls — worth reading as lessons, not just as caveats.

FindingDetailThe general lesson
Test-time input synthesized from the answerOn ifc_raw HF-only test splits, the LF-consuming families (Transolver/v9) receive "LF" made by bilinearly downsampling the HF ground truth — a blurred copy of the very field being predicted.Always ask where each test-time input comes from. An input derived from the label flatters the score — a subtle cousin of data leakage.
Name ≠ implementation"batchens" is a multi-head ensemble (not rank-1 BatchEnsemble); "cqr" is split-conformal σ-scaling (not textbook CQR) and spends 20% of the scarce HF data on calibration; IQN uses a constant τ channel (not the paper's cosine embedding); "FiLM fine-tune" trains all weights.Read the code, not the paper name in the config. Ablation tables are only as honest as the mechanisms actually implemented.
Checkpoint-resume gapsThe 9 shared-runner FIRE families ignore --ckpt_dir entirely; the transfer/DINO/coreg-smoke families checkpoint only at stage end. Proper mid-run resume exists only in fno_coreg_residual, fno_mf_stack, both Transolvers, and v9.On preemptible compute, resume logic is not plumbing — it is budget. A job that restarts from epoch 0 after every preemption may effectively never finish.
